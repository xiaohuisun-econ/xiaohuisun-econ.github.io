Job market paper

Business Taxation, Firm Dynamics, and Heterogeneous Policy Impacts

This paper studies how business income taxation affects firm dynamics across industries differing in capital intensity. Using the German IAB Establishment Panel and district-level variation in local trade tax rates, I find that higher taxes are associated with greater establishment exit, with an effect twice as large among capital-intensive establishments as in the full sample. I develop a two-sector general equilibrium model with endogenous entry and exit, heterogeneous productivity, and capital accumulation. Incomplete capital-cost deductibility makes capital-intensive firms more sensitive to taxation. Calibrated to German moments, the model reproduces the empirical exit pattern when prices are held fixed. In the long run, equilibrium price adjustments reverse this response, reducing entry and exit rates. Firm mass contracts more in capital-intensive industries, while aggregate employment, consumption, and productivity fall. Lower entry costs increase firm creation and output. These findings highlight how capital intensity and equilibrium adjustments shape the effects of business taxation.

Heterogeneous job loss risk and precautionary savings with financial frictions

Revise and resubmit at Macroeconomic Dynamics

This paper studies how heterogeneity in job loss risk across industries and regions affects households’ saving behavior and welfare in an economy with incomplete markets. Using U.S. micro-level data, I show that manufacturing and construction industries exhibit significantly higher separation rates, and their uneven spatial concentration correlates with regional disparities in savings incentives. Motivated by these facts, I develop a heterogeneous-agent model in which unemployment risk depends jointly on workers’ industries of employment and regions of residence, and the risk-free rate is endogenously determined. With parameters estimated using U.S. data, the model shows that the variations in risk exposure are large enough to result in significantly more savings in risky states and industries, as well as varying welfare consequences across subgroups. In a counterfactual policy experiment that tightens credit limits, the endogenous decline in the risk-free rate partially offsets welfare losses for the constrained households while widening welfare differences across regions.

The Shift from Traditional Pensions to 401(k)s: Retirement Risks and the Timing of Retirement

With Rosemary Kaiser and Yang Xuan (Submitted)

U.S. retirement coverage has shifted from defined benefit (DB) to defined contribution (DC) plans. We build a life-cycle model in which DB and DC plans differ in exposure to longevity and return risk and households respond to the additional risks by saving more and working longer. These two insurance margins interact with one another: additional saving before retirement reduces the need for precautionary labor supply at older ages. The model matches observed differences in retirement timing across plan types and decomposes the effects of the transition into plan structure and generosity, return risk, and longevity risk. Return risk is the main driver of delayed retirement, while longevity risk is the main source of the welfare loss attributable to uncertainty. We then evaluate annuitization as a way to improve the insurance provided by accumulated assets. Allowing retirees to convert assets into actuarially fair annuities raises welfare and induces earlier retirement, with larger gains after the shift toward DC plans.
